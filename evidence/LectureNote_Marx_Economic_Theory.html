Marx's View of the Economy

I. The Starting Point: The Commodity

Marx opens Capital with the simplest observable unit of capitalist economic life: the commodity. "The wealth of those societies in which the capitalist mode of production prevails, presents itself as 'an immense accumulation of commodities,' its unit being a single commodity" (Capital I, p. 27). Every commodity has a dual character. It has a use-value --- it satisfies some human want, whether of the stomach or the fancy. And it has an exchange-value --- a quantitative ratio in which it trades against other commodities. Twenty yards of linen exchange for one coat, or for ten pounds of tea, or for two ounces of gold.

The question that drives Marx's entire theory is deceptively simple: what makes these heterogeneous objects commensurable? What is the common substance that allows us to equate linen, coats, and tea in definite proportions? Marx's answer: labour. Not the idiosyncratic labour of a particular worker on a particular day, but socially necessary labour time --- the labour required to produce a commodity "under the normal conditions of production, and with the average degree of skill and intensity prevalent at the time" (CI, p. 28). A commodity's value is a crystallization of abstract human labour.

This yields Marx's general law of value, stated with elegant simplicity in Value, Price and Profit:

The values of commodities are directly as the times of labor employed in their production, and are inversely as the productive powers of the labor employed. (VPP, p. 65)

If a technological improvement halves the labour time needed to produce linen, the value of linen halves, regardless of how much labour was actually spent producing any particular bolt. Value is a social relation, not a physical property.

II. Money and the Expression of Value

Value does not walk around with a label on its forehead. It must express itself through exchange. Marx traces this expression through increasingly general forms. In the elementary form, 20 yards of linen = 1 coat: one commodity serves as a mirror in which another sees its own value reflected. In the expanded form, linen expresses its value against an entire list of commodities. In the general form, all commodities express their value in a single universal equivalent. When that universal equivalent becomes gold (or paper backed by convention), we have the money form.

Money is not an arbitrary convention, in Marx's account. It is the commodity that, by social practice, has been singled out to serve as the universal equivalent --- the form in which the abstract labour embodied in all other commodities can be measured and compared. Foley presents this progression cleanly through a series of equations (Foley, pp. 18--19), showing how each successive form resolves an inadequacy in the previous one.

III. The Circuit of Capital: M--C--M'

With commodities and money in hand, Marx makes a crucial distinction between two kinds of circulation. In simple commodity circulation, a producer sells what he has made in order to buy what he needs: C--M--C. The weaver sells cloth for money and buys bread. Money here is a transient medium; the purpose of the circuit is consumption.

Capital moves in the opposite direction: M--C--M. Money is advanced to buy commodities, which are then sold for money again. But this circuit would be pointless if the capitalist got back only what he started with. "The exact form of this process is therefore M--C--M', where M' = M + $\Delta$M = the original sum advanced, plus an increment. This increment or excess over the original value I call 'surplus-value.' The value originally advanced, therefore, not only remains intact while in circulation, but adds to itself a surplus-value or expands itself. It is this movement that converts it into capital" (CI, p. 106).

Capital, then, is not a thing --- it is value in process, money in process, constantly changing from one form to the other "without thereby becoming lost, and thus assumes an automatically active character" (CI, p. 107). The capitalist is merely the conscious bearer of this process. His objective function is the endless augmentation of value.

In its more complete form, the circuit is:

$$M ;-; C{MP, LP} ;\cdots; (P) ;\cdots; C' ;-; M' = M + \Delta M$$

where $MP$ denotes means of production, $LP$ denotes labour-power, and $(P)$ denotes the production process itself (Foley, p. 33). Money is advanced to purchase inputs; these inputs are consumed in production; the resulting commodities are sold for more money than was originally advanced. The question that haunts all of classical political economy is: where does the increment come from?

IV. The Secret of Surplus Value

Marx insists that surplus value cannot arise from exchange. If commodities exchange at their values, no one gains at another's expense --- exchange is a transfer, not a creation, of value. "Turn and twist then as we may, the fact remains unaltered. If equivalents are exchanged, no surplus-value results, and if non-equivalents are exchanged, still no surplus-value. Circulation, or the exchange of commodities, begets no value" (CI, p. 114). Cheating one buyer simply enriches one capitalist at the expense of another; it cannot explain a surplus for the capitalist class as a whole.

The solution is that the capitalist finds on the market a peculiar commodity whose use-value has the special property of being a source of value --- and of more value than it costs. That commodity is labour-power: not labour itself (which is an activity, not a thing that can be bought), but the worker's capacity to labour for a given period.

"What the working man sells is not directly his Labour, but his Laboring Power, the temporary disposal of which he makes over to the capitalist" (VPP, p. 72). The value of labour-power, like that of any commodity, is determined by the labour time required for its production --- that is, by the labour embodied in the food, clothing, housing, and other necessaries required "to produce, develop, maintain, and perpetuate the laboring power" (CI, p. 121).

Here is the crux. Suppose the value of a day's labour-power equals 6 hours of labour (embodied in 3 shillings' worth of necessaries). The capitalist pays the worker 3 shillings and acquires the right to use his labour-power for an entire working day. If the working day is 12 hours, the worker labours 6 hours to reproduce the equivalent of his own wages --- and 6 hours beyond that, for which he receives no equivalent. This unpaid labour is the source of surplus value.

Marx works through a detailed spinning example in Capital I, Chapters 7--9. The capitalist lays out:

Input Cost
10 lbs cotton 10s (constant capital)
Spindle wear 2s (constant capital)
1 day's labour-power 3s (variable capital)
Total 15s

If the worker works only 6 hours, he spins 10 lbs of cotton into yarn, adding 3s of new value --- exactly matching his wages. The product is worth 15s, and there is no surplus. "The trick has not come off" (CI, pp. 133--134). But the capitalist did not buy 6 hours of labour; he bought a day's labour-power. He makes the worker spin for 12 hours. Now the worker adds 6s of new value while consuming 20 lbs of cotton (20s) and wearing down spindles (4s). The product is worth 30s; the outlay was 27s; the surplus is 3s.

The beauty of the analysis is that no fraud has occurred. Every commodity --- cotton, spindles, labour-power, yarn --- has been bought and sold at its value. Yet a surplus has materialized, because the value that labour-power creates during a full working day exceeds the value that labour-power costs. As Marx puts it: "The capitalist would consequently realize a profit of three shillings, not by selling his commodity at a price over and above its value, but by selling it at its real value" (VPP, p. 87).

V. Constant Capital, Variable Capital, and the Anatomy of Value

Marx decomposes the value of any commodity into three parts:

$$W = c + v + s$$

where $c$ is constant capital (the value of raw materials, machinery, and other means of production that is merely transferred to the product), $v$ is variable capital (the value of labour-power, which is reproduced plus an additional increment), and $s$ is surplus value (the value created by labour beyond the reproduction of its own cost).

The terminology is precise. Constant capital is called "constant" because it does not change its magnitude in the production process --- it reappears in the product, no more, no less. Variable capital is called "variable" because it does change: the worker receives $v$ in wages but creates $v + s$ in new value.

From this decomposition flow two key ratios. The rate of surplus value (or rate of exploitation):

$$e = \frac{s}{v}$$

This measures the division of the working day between paid and unpaid labour. If the rate is 100%, the worker works half the day for himself and half for the capitalist. Foley provides a striking empirical estimate for U.S. manufacturing in 1974: $e = 2.51$, meaning workers received roughly one dollar for every $3.51 of value they created (Foley, p. 46).

The rate of profit:

$$r = \frac{s}{c + v}$$

This is what the capitalist actually cares about --- his return on total capital advanced. The fundamental identity connecting the two is:

$$r = e \cdot k \quad \text{where } k = \frac{v}{c + v}$$

The rate of profit equals the rate of exploitation times the share of variable capital in total capital (Foley, p. 45). This identity is the hinge on which Marx's crisis theory turns, as we shall see.

VI. Accumulation: "That is Moses and the Prophets!"

Capital is not content with a single circuit. The surplus value generated in production is not (or not entirely) consumed by the capitalist --- it is reinvested, becoming additional capital that generates additional surplus value. "Accumulate, accumulate! That is Moses and the prophets!" (CI, p. 416).

The process of reproduction on an expanding scale means that capitalism is inherently a system of growth. Each round of production generates surplus value; a portion of that surplus is ploughed back as additional constant and variable capital; the enlarged capital generates a still-larger surplus; and so on. Marx works through a spinning-mill example: a capitalist with 10,000 pounds of capital (8,000 constant, 2,000 variable) generates 2,000 pounds of surplus at a 100% rate of exploitation. If he reinvests half, the cycle expands by 1,000 pounds --- 800 additional constant capital, 200 additional variable capital --- and the next round generates even more surplus (CI, pp. 408--416).

But accumulation is not merely a quantitative expansion. It reproduces the capital relation itself. "Capitalist production, therefore, under its aspect of a continuous connected process, of a process of reproduction, produces not only commodities, not only surplus-value, but it also produces and reproduces the capitalist relation; on the one side the capitalist, on the other the wage labourer" (CI, p. 405). The worker consumes his wages, appears again the next morning with nothing to sell but his labour-power, and the cycle repeats. Capitalism is a self-reproducing social system.

VII. The Organic Composition of Capital and the Tendency of the Rate of Profit to Fall

As capitalism develops, technical progress leads capitalists to replace living labour with machinery. The ratio of constant to variable capital --- what Marx calls the organic composition of capital, $c/v$ --- tends to rise over time. More and more of each dollar of capital goes to plant, equipment, and raw materials; less and less goes to wages.

Recall that only variable capital generates surplus value. If $c/v$ rises while the rate of exploitation ($s/v$) remains constant, the rate of profit must fall. Marx demonstrates this with a simple numerical table in Capital III:

Stage $c$ $v$ $s$ (at $e=100%$) Total capital Rate of profit
I 50 100 100 150 66.7%
II 100 100 100 200 50.0%
III 200 100 100 300 33.3%
IV 300 100 100 400 25.0%
V 400 100 100 500 20.0%

(CIII, pp. 153--154)

As the organic composition rises from 50:100 to 400:100, the rate of profit falls from 66.7% to 20%, even though the rate of exploitation is unchanged. This is the law of the tendential fall in the rate of profit --- the single most consequential and controversial proposition in Marx's economics.

The word "tendential" is critical. Marx identifies six counteracting influences (CIII, Ch. 14): increasing intensity of exploitation, depression of wages below their value, cheapening of the elements of constant capital through technical progress itself, relative overpopulation (the "industrial reserve army"), foreign trade that provides cheap raw materials, and the growth of capital invested at interest rates below the average profit rate. These forces can retard, offset, or even temporarily reverse the tendency. Foley emphasizes that Marx's laws are not mechanical predictions but structural tendencies that assert themselves through their counteracting factors: "Even if the tendency for the rate of profit to fall is offset, say, by the appropriation of surplus value from colonial possessions, the significance of the basic law is unchanged" (Foley, p. 9).

There is a further subtlety. While the rate of profit falls, the mass of profit can rise simultaneously, because total capital grows faster than the rate declines. Marx gives an example: a capital of 6 million with an organic composition of 2:1 yields 2 million in profit at a 33% rate; a capital of 18 million with a composition of 5:1 yields 3 million at a 17% rate (CIII, pp. 155--157). The capitalist class as a whole is getting richer even as each dollar of capital earns less.

VIII. Crisis

The falling rate of profit does not simply slow things down. It generates crises --- periodic, violent interruptions in the process of accumulation. "It breeds over-production, speculation, crises, and surplus-capital alongside surplus-population" (CIII, p. 171).

The mechanism is as follows. The rate of profit is "the motive power of capitalist production. Things are produced only so long as they can be produced with a profit" (CIII, p. 171). As the rate falls, capital cannot find profitable investment outlets; idle capital accumulates. Simultaneously, workers are displaced by machinery and cannot find employment; idle labour accumulates. The paradox of crisis is the simultaneous existence of surplus capital and surplus population --- not because there is too much capital or too many workers in absolute terms, but because capital cannot be valorized at the required rate.

Credit amplifies the cycle. During the upswing, the credit system enables speculation far beyond the value of real commodities. Bills of exchange --- promises to pay arising from commodity sales on credit --- circulate as quasi-money, creating a chain of IOUs. Banks become "general managers of money-capital" (CIII, p. 277), centralizing idle reserves and channeling them into loans. In the speculative phase, "fictitious" bills with no underlying commodity transaction proliferate. When confidence breaks, the chain of credit collapses, and the crisis arrives not gradually but as a sudden rupture.

Marx does not present a single, mechanical crisis theory. His writings contain at least three interacting causal mechanisms: (1) the falling rate of profit squeezing investment incentives, (2) the overproduction of commodities that cannot be sold at profitable prices because workers' purchasing power is limited to their wages, and (3) the instability of the credit system that amplifies both the boom and the bust. These are not alternative theories but complementary dimensions of the same structural contradiction: capitalism's drive to expand production beyond the limits set by the conditions of valorization.

IX. Absolute and Relative Surplus Value

Marx distinguishes two ways in which surplus value can be increased.

Absolute surplus value is extracted by lengthening the working day. If the value of labour-power requires 6 hours to reproduce, extending the working day from 10 to 12 hours increases surplus labour from 4 to 6 hours. This was the dominant mode in early capitalism --- the factory legislation of the 1830s--1860s, which Marx documents extensively, was a political struggle over the length of the working day.

Relative surplus value is extracted by reducing the value of labour-power itself. If technical progress in agriculture and consumer-goods industries lowers the cost of workers' necessaries, the same real standard of living can be reproduced in fewer hours of labour. The working day stays the same, but a larger fraction of it becomes surplus labour. This is the dominant mode in mature capitalism, and it explains why the rate of exploitation can rise even as real wages increase.

Foley presents this with a clear diagram (p. 50--51). The working day is divided into necessary labour time (which reproduces the value of labour-power) and surplus labour time (which is appropriated by the capitalist). Absolute surplus value stretches the total bar; relative surplus value shifts the dividing line leftward.

X. Prices of Production and the Equalization of the Rate of Profit

In Capital Volume III, Marx addresses a problem that his critics had long identified: if value is determined by labour time, why do industries with different organic compositions of capital earn the same rate of profit? A labour-intensive industry (low $c/v$) should, other things equal, produce more surplus value per dollar of capital than a capital-intensive one (high $c/v$). But competition ensures that capital flows toward high-return sectors and away from low-return ones, equalizing returns across the economy.

Marx's solution is the concept of prices of production. Individual commodities do not sell at their values; they sell at prices that yield the average rate of profit on the capital advanced. Industries with above-average organic composition sell above value; those with below-average composition sell below value. The total surplus value produced in the economy is redistributed through competition so that each capitalist receives a share proportional to his total capital, not to his variable capital alone. At the aggregate level, total prices equal total values and total profits equal total surplus value --- the redistribution is internal to the capitalist class.

XI. The Broader Vision

Marx's economics is not merely a theory of prices and profits. It is a theory of how a specific form of social organization --- one in which the means of production are privately owned and labour-power is a commodity --- generates its own laws of motion, its own characteristic pathologies, and ultimately its own historical limits.

The drive to accumulate pushes capitalists to revolutionize the forces of production continuously. But each revolution raises the organic composition of capital, presses on the rate of profit, and intensifies the contradictions that erupt periodically as crises. The centralization of capital --- as larger firms swallow smaller ones --- concentrates economic power in fewer hands. The socialization of the labour process --- as production becomes ever more collective, coordinated, and interdependent --- stands in growing tension with the private appropriation of its fruits.

Marx saw this tension as ultimately irresolvable within the framework of capitalist property relations. "The monopoly of capital becomes a fetter upon the mode of production, which has sprung up and flourished along with, and under it. Centralisation of the means of production and socialisation of labour at last reach a point where they become incompatible with their capitalist integument. This integument is burst asunder. The knell of capitalist private property sounds. The expropriators are expropriated" (CI, pp. 539--540).

Whether one accepts this conclusion or not, the analytical apparatus Marx constructs --- the circuit of capital, the distinction between constant and variable capital, the rate of surplus value, the organic composition, the tendential fall of the rate of profit, the role of credit in amplifying instability --- remains one of the most ambitious and internally coherent frameworks ever devised for understanding how a capitalist economy functions, grows, and periodically collapses.

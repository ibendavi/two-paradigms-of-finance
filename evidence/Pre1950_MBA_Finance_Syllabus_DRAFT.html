FINANCE CORE 101: How Finance Was Actually Taught Before Modigliani-Miller

Key Finding

The pre-1950 textbook canon is remarkably unified. Mead (1910, Wharton), Dewing (1920, Harvard), Lyon (1912, Dartmouth/Tuck), Lough (1917, NYU), Gerstenberg (1924, used "for more than thirty years"), Guthmann & Dougall (1940, Northwestern), and Bosland (1949, Brown) all teach essentially the same framework:

  1. Value = Capitalized Earnings (P = E/r, where r comes from comparables)
  2. Capital structure exists to exploit the spread between the earnings yield and the borrowing rate ("trading on the equity")
  3. Dividends are a practical decision about how much to retain vs. distribute
  4. The manager's job is to raise capital, invest it productively, manage income, and distribute profits -- all in service of maximizing the earning power of the enterprise

This is exactly the "EPS paradigm" described in the Ben-David & Chinco research program.

A 12-Session MBA Syllabus Built Entirely on Pre-1950 College Textbooks

Instructor: Professor Itzhak Ben-David, Fisher College of Business, Ohio State University

Course Philosophy: Before 1958, every finance textbook taught students that the value of a business arises from its earning power, and that this earning power is capitalized at a rate drawn from comparable enterprises. Managers maximized earnings per share; investors priced securities by capitalizing current earnings. This course reconstructs that original curriculum using the actual textbooks from the era. Students will discover that the "practitioner paradigm" of earnings capitalization -- which MM's irrelevance theorem was supposed to replace -- never actually disappeared from business practice.

SESSION 1: The Corporation and Its Securities

Pre-1950 title: "The Instruments of Corporation Finance"

Key concepts: The corporation as a legal fiction; forms of business organization; the charter and powers of incorporation; common stock, preferred stock, and bonds as the "instruments" of corporate finance; the distinction between owned and borrowed capital.

Required readings:

  • Ly Ch I: "The Instruments of Corporation Finance" (pp. 1-49) -- How corporate securities meet the "differing desires of capitalists" for risk, income, and control
  • G Ch 1-4: "The Corporation as a Form of Organization" through "Corporate Management and Control"
  • Bo Ch 1-4: "Introduction" through "Organizing the Corporation"

Alternative readings:

  • M Ch I: "Introduction" (pp. 1-13)
  • L Ch II-III: "Forms of Business Enterprises" and "The Corporation" (pp. 11-44)
  • D Vol I, Ch I: "Capitalization and Common Stocks"

Discussion questions:

  1. Mead (1910) opens by noting $34.7 billion in corporate securities outstanding. What does the framing of "corporation finance" as the study of these securities tell us about how the field was conceptualized -- versus the modern framing as "maximizing firm value"?
  2. Lyon says corporate securities are designed to "meet the differing desires of capitalists... from the standpoint of risk, income, and control." How does this investor-centric framing compare to MM's framework where capital structure is about firm value?
  3. Bosland (1949) notes that 82% of all corporations have assets under $250,000. Is "corporation finance" really about large public firms, or about ALL corporations?

SESSION 2: Capitalization and the Earnings Theory of Value

Pre-1950 title: "The Basis of Capitalization"

Key concepts: Three bases of capitalization (investment/cost, initial expenses/losses, and earning power); the earnings theory of capitalization vs. the cost theory; estimating probable earnings; choosing the rate of capitalization from comparable enterprises; overcapitalization and watered stock.

Required readings:

  • L Ch VIII: "Basis of Capitalization" (pp. 172-200) -- THE key chapter. Three bases of capitalization; capitalization of earning power; estimates of earning power; adjusting capitalization to assets; valuation of goodwill; capitalization of public service companies
  • G Ch 9: "Capitalization and Capital Structure" -- The earnings theory of capitalization; the cost theory of capitalization; estimating probable earnings and the rate of capitalization; trading on the equity
  • Ly Ch III: "Watered Stock" (pp. 83-107) -- Capitalization in relation to assets; objections to stock-watering; as fraud on the buyer; satisfying various interests

Alternative readings:

  • B Ch I: "Methods of Appraising Property Values: Industrial" (pp. 1-16) -- "Value of capital goods arises largely from income they produce; the capitalization method of valuing industrial securities is sound"
  • GD Ch 8: "Promotional Finance and Overcapitalization" (compare Bosland's version)
  • Bo Ch 7-8: "Promotion and the Financial Plan" and "Promotional Finance and Overcapitalization"

Discussion questions:

  1. Lough presents THREE bases of capitalization: investment (cost), initial expenses, and earning power. He concludes that earning power is the correct basis. Why? And how does this compare to the modern "book value vs. market value" debate?
  2. Gerstenberg says the "earnings theory" determines capitalization by "capitalizing the probable net earnings at the prevailing rate for the class of business." This is P = E/r. What determines r in this framework? Is it the same as the "cost of equity" in CAPM?
  3. The concept of "watered stock" (capitalization exceeding the earning power of the enterprise) was a major public policy concern before 1930. Why has this concept disappeared from modern finance?

SESSION 3: Trading on the Equity -- The Original Capital Structure Theory

Pre-1950 title: "Trading on the Equity"

Key concepts: The principle that borrowing at a fixed rate below the earnings yield on total capital amplifies the return to common stockholders; preferred stock as a means of trading on the equity; the operating ratio and its effect on the variation of net earnings; the limits of safe borrowing; how minority shareholders can control a corporation through leverage.

Required readings:

  • Ly Ch II: "Trading on the Equity" (pp. 50-82) -- 50 pages devoted entirely to this concept. Capitalization in relation to earnings; principles governing the borrowing of capital in business; the common shareholder as proprietor; borrowing limited by liability of net earnings to fluctuate; variation of net earnings depends on the operating ratio; preferred stock as a means of trading on the equity; how a minority can control a corporation
  • G Ch 9 (sections on "Trading on the equity") -- The earnings theory applied to leverage decisions
  • GD Ch 9: "Factors Determining the Form of Capital Structure" (pp. 153-180) -- Rate of return earned upon investment; stability of earnings; trading on the equity

Alternative readings:

  • D Vol I, Ch I-II: Common Stocks and Bonds -- how leverage works through the capital structure
  • L Ch V-VII: "Owned Capital," "Borrowed Capital -- Short-Term," "Borrowed Capital -- Long-Term" (pp. 64-168) -- The complete menu of corporate securities
  • Bo Ch 5-6: "Common and Preferred Stock" and "Corporate Bonds"

Discussion questions:

  1. Lyon's entire Chapter II (50 pages) is called "Trading on the Equity." This is the pre-MM capital structure theory. State it precisely: when is it profitable to issue debt?
  2. Lyon shows that the variability of net earnings depends on the operating ratio (fixed costs / total costs). This is what we now call "operating leverage." How does operating leverage constrain financial leverage in this framework?
  3. In the modern curriculum, capital structure theory begins with MM's irrelevance proposition, then adds "frictions" (taxes, bankruptcy costs, etc.) to explain why leverage exists. In the pre-1950 curriculum, leverage exists because the earnings yield exceeds the borrowing rate. Which starting point is more useful for a practitioner?

SESSION 4: Corporate Securities -- Bonds, Preferred Stock, and Their Valuation

Pre-1950 title: "The Valuation of Fixed-Income Securities"

Key concepts: Types of corporate bonds (mortgage, debenture, collateral trust, income, convertible); bond valuation by yield method; the concept of "investment value" vs. "speculative value"; bond safety standards (earnings coverage, asset coverage); preferred stock characteristics and valuation.

Required readings:

  • GrD Part III, Ch 22-24: "The Selection of Fixed-Income Securities" and "Specific Standards for Bond Investment" (pp. 281-325) -- Safety criteria: earnings coverage, asset coverage, stock-equity ratio
  • B Ch IV-VI: "Bond Valuation: Investment Bonds," "Bond Valuation: Speculative Bonds," "Bond Valuation: Speculative Bonds Further Considered" (pp. 41-78) -- Yield method; defaulting bond values depend on earning capacity; rates used for capitalizing available earnings
  • B Ch VII-IX: "Valuation of Preferred Stocks: Investment Issues" through "Speculative Issues Further Considered" (pp. 79-106) -- Earning power as the key determinant; analogy between investment preferred stocks and bonds

Alternative readings:

  • D Vol I, Ch II-VII: Complete treatment of every type of corporate bond and preferred stock
  • L Ch VII: "Borrowed Capital -- Long-Term" (pp. 130-168) -- Bonds and mortgages, sinking funds, safeguarding bonds
  • GD Ch 6: "Corporate Bonds" (pp. 86-115)

Discussion questions:

  1. Badger says: "Defaulting bond values may depend on earning capacity; rates used for capitalizing available earnings into bond values." He values distressed bonds by capitalizing their likely income stream. How does this differ from modern approaches to distressed debt?
  2. Graham & Dodd propose specific quantitative standards for bond safety (e.g., minimum earnings coverage ratios). In modern finance, bond pricing comes from term structure models and credit risk models. Which approach does a credit analyst actually use today?
  3. Badger treats investment-grade preferred stock as essentially equivalent to a bond (valued by yield comparison). When does preferred stock become "speculative" and require a different valuation method?

SESSION 5: The Valuation of Common Stocks -- Capitalizing Earnings

Pre-1950 title: "Capitalization Rates for Common Stocks"

Key concepts: Common stock earnings defined (the "residual" claim); the straight capitalization method (P = E / r); determining the appropriate capitalization rate from market comparables; how risk factors (industry, stability, management, liquidity) affect the rate; the relationship between book value and market value; goodwill as capitalized excess earnings.

Required readings:

  • B Ch X-XII: "Common Stock Earnings Defined," "Capitalization Rates for Common Stocks," "Risk Factors Affecting Capitalization Rates" (pp. 107-136) -- The pure earnings capitalization approach. "How to analyze active securities; ratio of earnings to market prices for typical stocks; United States Circuit Court recommends 15% ratio; rates normally looked for by investors in common stocks; risk factors will cause rate to vary"
  • GrD Part IV, Ch 30-33: "The Theory of Common-stock Investment: Historical Survey," "Current Modes of Common-stock Investment," "The Pros and Cons of the Valuation Approach," "Projections of Earnings and Dividends: Significance of the Earnings Record: The Concept of Earning Power" (pp. 385-428) -- Graham & Dodd's full framework for stock valuation
  • B Ch XIII-XIV: "Book Value vs. Market Value of Common Stocks" and "Good-will and Security Valuation" (pp. 137-159) -- Why book value bears no necessary relation to market value; appraisal by capitalization vs. goodwill method

Alternative readings:

  • GrD Ch 36-37: "The Capitalization Rate for Earnings and Dividends" and "Capitalization Structure" (pp. 454-474)
  • L Ch XXII: "Financial Standards" (pp. 500-524) -- Stock and bond issues in relation to gross earnings; operating ratios; comparative analysis
  • Ly Ch VII: "The Market and the Price" (pp. 200-219)

Discussion questions:

  1. Badger states that "the ratio of earnings to market prices for typical stocks" is the key input for valuation. This is the E/P ratio (earnings yield). He notes the Circuit Court recommends a 15% capitalization rate for industrials. What is the equivalent E/P ratio for the S&P 500 today? What does the difference tell us?
  2. Graham & Dodd distinguish between "earning power" (normal, sustainable earnings) and reported earnings. Why does this distinction matter for the capitalization method? What is the modern equivalent?
  3. Badger's thesis (from the preface): "The exchange value of all capital goods arises out of earning capacity... security values arise from capitalized earnings." Is there ANY modern finance textbook that states this as its central thesis? Why or why not?

SESSION 6: The Determination and Management of Corporate Income

Pre-1950 title: "The Administration of Income"

Key concepts: The formula for net income; operating expenses vs. capital expenditures; depreciation and wasting assets; the honest statement of gross earnings; reserves (sinking fund, depreciation, contingency); surplus and its management; hidden surpluses; the income account vs. the balance sheet as the key financial statement.

Required readings:

  • D Vol III, Ch I-III: "The Theory of Accounting as Applied to Corporation Finance," "Compensation for Wasting Assets" (depreciation), "Cost of Borrowed Capital"
  • L Ch XVIII: "Determination of Net Income" (pp. 415-434) -- Formula for income; honesty in stating gross earnings; operating expenses and deductions; reserves; operating expenses and betterments
  • GrD Part II, Ch 9-15: "Analysis of the Income Account" through "Reported Earnings and Taxable Earnings" (pp. 84-174) -- Effect of reserves, inventories, depreciation, amortization on reported earnings

Alternative readings:

  • M Ch XIV-XV: "The Determination of Profits" and "The Determination of Profits -- Depreciation" (pp. 169-208)
  • GrD Ch 16-18: "Balance-sheet Analysis," "Per-share Figures and Key Ratios," "A Comparison Based on Key Ratios" (pp. 175-228) -- Financial statement analysis
  • G Ch 16-18: "Financial Reports," "Income Statement," "Surplus Account and Statement of Financial Position"

Discussion questions:

  1. Dewing devotes an entire volume (Vol III) to "Administration of Income." In modern textbooks, this material belongs to accounting, not finance. Why were these considered the same field before 1950?
  2. Lough warns about "concealment of betterment expenditures" -- charging capital expenditures to operating expenses to understate earnings. This is the opposite of the modern concern about capitalizing expenses to overstate earnings. What changed?
  3. Graham & Dodd (1951) place income-account analysis BEFORE security valuation (Parts I-II before Parts III-IV). In the 1934 edition, it was the reverse. Why did they make this change?

SESSION 7: Dividend Policy and the Distribution of Profits

Pre-1950 title: "Fixing the Proportion of Profits to be Paid Out in Dividends"

Key concepts: Net income and dividends; average rates of dividends; percentages of earnings devoted to dividends; regularity of dividends desirable; variability of profits; rule for maintaining regularity; cash requirements for dividends; paying dividends from accumulated surplus; stock dividends; scrip dividends; legal rules affecting dividends.

Required readings:

  • M Ch XVII: "Fixing the Proportion of Profits to be Paid Out in Dividends" (pp. 226-238) -- Exactly what the title says: a practical guide to setting the payout ratio
  • L Ch XIX: "Dividends" (pp. 435-464) -- Net income and dividends; average rates; percentages of earnings devoted to dividends; regularity desirable; policies of important companies; paying from accumulated surplus; effects of lack of prudence; scrip dividends; stock dividends; legal rules
  • GD Ch 22: "Reserves and Dividend Policy" (pp. 418-448) -- 10 factors determining dividend payments

Alternative readings:

  • D Vol III, Ch VI-VII: "Distribution of Profits to Stockholders" and "Practical Expediency of Dividend Disbursements"
  • Bo Ch 13: "Dividend Policy" (pp. 265-289)
  • GrD Part VI, Ch 48: "Stockholders, Managements, and Dividend Policy" (pp. 586-598)
  • M Ch XVIII: "The Methods of Distributing the Surplus" (pp. 239-252)

Discussion questions:

  1. Mead's chapter title says it all: "Fixing the Proportion of Profits to be Paid Out in Dividends." This is a managerial DECISION, not a theoretical puzzle. How does this framing differ from the modern "dividend puzzle" (Miller & Modigliani 1961)?
  2. Lough devotes 30 pages to the PRACTICAL considerations of dividend policy: regularity, cash requirements, accumulated surplus, legal rules. None of these appear in modern dividend theory. Why not?
  3. Guthmann & Dougall list 10 factors determining dividend payments. Compare these to the modern theories: signaling, agency costs, clientele effects, catering. How much overlap is there?

SESSION 8: Raising Capital -- Promotion, Underwriting, and Securities Markets

Pre-1950 title: "Securing Capital"

Key concepts: The three steps of promotion (investigation, assembling, financing); the promoter's role and compensation; the financial plan for a new enterprise; underwriting syndicates; methods of selling securities; the prospectus; investment banking; privileged subscriptions (rights offerings).

Required readings:

  • L Ch X-XIII: "Promotion," "The Promoter," "Promoting Combinations," "Selling Securities Direct" (pp. 229-318)
  • D Vol II, Ch I-IV: "The Work of the Promoter," "The Banker's Contribution to Promotion," "The Principles of Financial Plans," "Financial Plan for a Manufacturing Enterprise"
  • GD Ch 10: "The Sale of Securities and Investment Banking" (pp. 176-209)

Alternative readings:

  • M Ch II-VI: "The Work of the Promoter" through "State Supervision of Security Issues"
  • L Ch XIV-XV: "Selling Securities Through Dealers" and "Underwriting" (pp. 319-358)
  • Bo Ch 7, 10: "Promotion and the Financial Plan" and "The Sale of Securities and Investment Banking"
  • G Ch 11-12: "Promotion and the Financial Plan" and "Underwriting"

Discussion questions:

  1. In pre-1950 textbooks, "Promotion" (the founding and initial financing of corporations) occupies 2-4 chapters. In modern textbooks, the equivalent topic (IPOs) gets perhaps 10 pages. What has been lost?
  2. The "financial plan" -- choosing the right mix of stocks and bonds for a new enterprise -- is treated as a DESIGN problem, not an optimization problem. How does this compare to modern capital structure theory?
  3. Lough notes that "underwriting has become so important that no large issue of securities is sold without it." How does this role differ from modern bookbuilding?

SESSION 9: Working Capital and Short-Term Finance

Pre-1950 title: "Internal Financial Management"

Key concepts: Fixed capital vs. working capital; estimating capital requirements; the investment of capital funds; trade credit, bank credit, and short-term notes; the management of cash and cash resources; budgets; seasonal variations in working capital; turnover ratios.

Required readings:

  • L Ch XVI-XVII: "Investment of Capital Funds" and "Calculating Requirements for Working Capital" (pp. 355-414)
  • L Ch XXI: "Budgets" (pp. 482-499)
  • GD Ch 12: "Financing Operations" (pp. 240-264)

Alternative readings:

  • G Ch 15-16: "Working Capital" and "Financing Current Operations"
  • GD Ch 18-20: "Short-Term Financing" (three chapters, pp. 299-367)
  • D Vol III, Ch IV: "The Management of Surplus"

Discussion questions:

  1. Lough devotes an entire chapter to "month-by-month calculations" of working capital needs. Modern finance has largely ceded this to accounting and operations. Should MBA students learn it?
  2. The concept of "turnover" (sales / capital employed) was central to pre-1950 financial analysis. DuPont decomposition (ROE = margin x turnover x leverage) codified this in 1914. Is turnover analysis still useful?
  3. Lough presents budgeting as a FINANCE topic. Today it belongs to managerial accounting. Where should it be taught?

SESSION 10: Expansion, Combination, and the Holding Company

Pre-1950 title: "Expansion and Consolidation"

Key concepts: Motives for expansion (the "law of balanced return"); industrial combinations and consolidations; the holding company; mergers vs. consolidations vs. acquisitions; the financial treatment of goodwill in combinations; "community of interests"; the trust problem.

Required readings:

  • D Vol IV, Ch I-III: "The Underlying Motives of Expansion," "The Law of Balanced Return," "Industrial Combinations"
  • M Ch XXVIII-XXIX: "Consolidation of Corporations" and "The Holding Company" (pp. 336-372)
  • Bo Ch 15-17: "Corporate Growth and Combination," "Corporate Concentration," "The Trust Problem" (pp. 312-416)

Alternative readings:

  • L Ch XII: "Promoting Combinations" (pp. 265-290)
  • GD Ch 25: "Mergers and Consolidation" (pp. 474-503)
  • D Vol IV, Ch IV-VI: "Expansion and Consolidation of Railroads," "The Public Utility Holding Company," "Community of Interests"

Discussion questions:

  1. Dewing's "Law of Balanced Return" -- that firms expand to equalize returns across related activities -- is an early theory of diversification. How does it compare to modern M&A theory?
  2. Pre-1950 textbooks devote extensive space to "the trust problem." This has largely disappeared from modern finance textbooks but returned as a public policy issue. Why did finance drop it?
  3. In a combination, how should the acquiring firm value the target? Pre-1950 answer: capitalize the target's earning power. Modern answer: DCF analysis. Are these different methods?

SESSION 11: Financial Failure, Receivership, and Reorganization

Pre-1950 title: "Failure and Reorganization"

Key concepts: Types of insolvency; causes of failure; receivership procedures; protective committees; the reorganization plan; the "absolute priority" rule; fair treatment of different classes of security holders.

Required readings:

  • D Vol V, Ch I-IV: "The Problem of Reorganization" through "Railroad Reorganization"
  • L Ch XXV-XXVI: "Insolvency and Receivership" and "Reorganization" (pp. 573-618)
  • GD Ch 14: "Corporate Failure and Reorganization" (pp. 290-311)

Alternative readings:

  • M Ch XXXI-XXXIII: "The Readjustment of the Capital Account" through "The Reorganization of Bankrupt Corporations" (pp. 383-450)
  • Bo Ch 14: "Corporate Failure and Reorganization" (pp. 290-311)
  • GrD Part VI, Ch 51: "Regulation by the SEC: Recapitalizations and Reorganizations" (pp. 621-635)

Discussion questions:

  1. Dewing's Vol V treats corporate distress as a normal phase of the business cycle, not as an aberration. How does this differ from the modern treatment of bankruptcy?
  2. The key question in reorganization: how do you reduce fixed charges while treating all classes of security holders fairly? How does the "absolute priority rule" relate to modern cramdown debates?
  3. Lough identifies "anxiety to pay dividends" as a cause of insolvency. Connect this to the EPS framework.

SESSION 12: The "Cost of Capital" Before Modigliani-Miller -- Synthesis

Pre-1950 title: "Capitalization, the Earnings Yield, and the Cost of Money"

Key concepts: The earnings yield (E/P) as the market's required return on equity; the interest rate as the cost of borrowed capital; "trading on the equity" as the fundamental capital structure principle; the rate of capitalization as determined by comparables, not by a risk model; the pre-MM synthesis.

Required readings:

  • B Preface + Ch XI: "Capitalization Rates for Common Stocks" (pp. 117-124)
  • G Ch 9: "Capitalization and Capital Structure" -- FULL chapter
  • GrD Ch 36-37: "The Capitalization Rate for Earnings and Dividends" and "Capitalization Structure" (pp. 454-474)
  • Ly Ch VIII: "Capitalization and the State" (pp. 220-265)

Alternative readings:

  • L Ch VIII: "Basis of Capitalization" (pp. 172-200) -- revisited as synthesis
  • D Vol III, Ch III: "Cost of Borrowed Capital"
  • GrD Ch 45: "Leverage" (pp. 556-562)
  • Bo Ch 9: "Typical Financial Plans" (pp. 153-175)

Discussion questions:

  1. Synthesize the pre-1950 framework: (a) How do investors value a firm? (b) How do managers choose capital structure? (c) What is the "cost of equity"? Compare each answer to the modern textbook.
  2. Is the earnings yield E/P the same concept as the CAPM's "required rate of return"? Why or why not?
  3. The big question: Did MM prove the practitioners wrong? Or did they change the objective function -- from maximizing EPS to maximizing firm value -- and thereby make a decision that was central under one objective irrelevant under another?

Problem Sets: Worked Numerical Exercises from Pre-1950 Textbooks

All exercises below come from actual pre-1950 textbooks in the collection. Page references are to the PDFs. The goldmine source is Dougall & Dauer, "One Hundred Short Problems in Corporation Finance" (1937, Northwestern), a dedicated problem book using real corporate data from Moody's Manuals.

Session 1-2 Problems: Corporation, Capitalization, Book Value

Problem 1 (Dougall #9): Balance Sheet Concepts Eagle Can Co.: Total assets $200,000. Capital: Common Stock $60K, 6% Preferred $40K, 5% Debentures $15K, Surplus $55K. Calculate: (a) capital, (b) capitalization, (c) capital structure ratios, (d) book value per share, (e) working capital, (f) net worth.

Problem 2 (Dougall #20): Book Value of Stock Company with 8% Preferred ($40M par), Class A and Class B no-par common. Given condensed balance sheet, calculate book value per share for each class.

Problem 3 (Gerstenberg p. 167): Earnings Capitalization A business earns $50,000/year. Fair capitalization rate is 8%. (a) What is the value? ($625,000) (b) Formula: V = E / r = E × multiplier, where multiplier = 100 / r

Problem 4 (Gerstenberg pp. 167-168): Capitalization Rate Negotiation A laundry earns $5,000/year. Seller asks $50,000 (10% return). Buyer considers 10% too low given the risk. They settle at $30,000 (16.7% return = 6× earnings). Discuss: what determines the "fair" capitalization rate?

Session 3 Problems: Trading on the Equity

Problem 5 (Gerstenberg pp. 173-174): Leverage Magnification — THE Key Exercise Company with $100K owned capital earns 10%, 15%, 3% in three years. Now add $100K borrowed at 5%.

  • Without leverage: Returns are 10%, 15%, 3%
  • With leverage: Returns become 15%, 25%, 1% Complete the full numerical table showing the magnification effect. Under what earnings rate does leverage hurt the common stockholder?

Problem 6 (Dougall #33): Multi-Layer Capital Structure Capital: $1M 6% first mortgage bonds, $500K 7% second mortgage bonds, $200K 6% income bonds, $500K 7% preferred, $2M common. Earnings before interest for 4 years: $192K, $100K, $150K, $385K. Calculate: % earned on common stock and total capitalization for each year.

Problem 7 (Guthmann & Dougall pp. 610-611): Holding Company Pyramiding Operating companies: $8M assets, $4.5M 5% bonds, $1.5M 6% preferred, $1.5M common, $500K surplus. Holding company buys all common ($2M) using: $750K 6% bonds, $500K 7% preferred, $750K common. (a) Minimum investment to control $8M of property? ($375,100 — less than 5%) (b) If operating companies earn 7% on assets ($560K): trace the flow through to holding company common. (Answer: 7% → 12% → 22% return magnification)

Session 4 Problems: Bond Valuation and Safety

Problem 8 (Badger Ch. V): Bond Capitalization "Total par value of new bonds = average earnings available / rate of capitalization" Apply to: Interboro Rapid Transit (earnings $X, cap rate 12-15%). What is the maximum safe bond issue?

Problem 9 (Guthmann & Dougall p. 301): Northern Pacific Railway — Real Data Total capitalization $557-565M (1928-1938). Capital stock $248M throughout. Rate earned on total: 6.3% (1928) declining to 1.8% (1938). Rate earned on stock: 8.5% (1928) to deficit in 1932 and 1938. Using the data, show how leverage magnifies both gains AND losses.

Session 5 Problems: Common Stock Valuation

Problem 10 (Dougall #60): Straight Earnings Capitalization Corporation with income before bond interest of $100,000. Capitalize at 20%. (a) What is the value? ($500,000) (b) What factors should affect the capitalization rate?

Problem 11 (Dougall #67): Over/Under-Capitalization — Full Case X Manufacturing Co.: Total assets $1.2M. Capital: Common $600K, 7% Debentures $500K, Surplus $150K. Average annual net earnings (5 years) before interest = $80,000. (a) Is the company over-, under-, or properly capitalized? (b) What would YOU pay for the enterprise? (c) How would your answer differ if this were a public utility?

Problem 12 (Gerstenberg pp. 467-468): M&A Valuation — Scientific Method Tangible assets = $100,000. Net earnings = $25,000/year. Capitalization rate = 10%. Capitalized earnings = $250,000. Goodwill = $250K - $100K = $150,000. Result: $100K preferred stock (for tangibles) + $150K common stock (for goodwill).

Problem 13 (Badger Ch. XI p. 120): Cost of Equity from Market Data 20 representative NYSE stocks (Jan 6, 1922): Average E/P = 18.8% (P/E ≈ 5.3×). This IS the cost of equity in the EPS paradigm. Compare to today's S&P 500 E/P of ~4-5%. What has changed?

Session 7 Problems: Dividend Policy

Problem 14 (Dougall #84): AT&T Dividend Decision — Extraordinary Real Data AT&T income account 1930-1934 (millions):

  • EPS: $10.44 (1930) declining to $6.52 (1934)
  • Dividends: $168M/year maintained throughout (9% annual rate since 1921)
  • 680,000 stockholders (1934) Full balance sheet and system statistics provided. Question: Should the directors continue, reduce, or omit the dividend? Argue both sides.

Problem 15 (Dougall #25): Cumulative Participating Preferred $100K common + $100K cumulative participating 7% preferred. Calculate dividend per share if distributing: (a) $18,000; (b) $5,000; (c) $62,000 after 5 years of passed dividends.

Session 10 Problems: Mergers and Combinations

Problem 16 (Dougall #91): Steel Company Merger Inland Steel + Joseph T. Ryerson merger (August 1935).

  • Inland: Net worth $55.5M, 1.2M shares, earnings 1929-1934 given
  • Ryerson: Net worth $11.2M, 400K shares, earnings 1929-1934 given
  • Price ranges for both stocks over 6 years Suggest an exchange ratio and state the criteria used.

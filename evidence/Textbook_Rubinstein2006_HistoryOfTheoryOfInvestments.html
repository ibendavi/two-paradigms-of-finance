Rubinstein (2006): A History of the Theory of Investments — The Annotated Intellectual Genealogy

Rubinstein, Mark. *A History of the Theory of Investments: My Annotated Bibliography.* Hoboken, NJ: John Wiley & Sons, 2006. 368 pages. (Rubinstein was professor of finance at UC Berkeley Haas School of Business.)

Key Finding

Mark Rubinstein's A History of the Theory of Investments (2006, Wiley Finance) is an annotated bibliography of ~500 key works in financial theory, organized in three periods: "Ancient" (pre-1950), "Classical" (1950-1980), and "Modern" (post-1980). It is the most comprehensive single-volume intellectual history of academic finance ever written, and essential for the EPS project because it documents which pre-1950 works the academic tradition chose to remember — and which it forgot. Crucially, Rubinstein reveals that Williams (1938) contained the MM capital structure irrelevance proposition 20 years before MM — and that Graham & Dodd (1934) nearly discovered it but turned away. The book also exposes Rubinstein's own framework: he privileges mathematical rigor, which systematically understates the role of practitioners who used the same ideas without formalization. The dominant corporate finance textbook tradition of earnings capitalization (Dewing, Guthmann, Solomon) is entirely absent from his history.

Details

The Three-Period Structure

Rubinstein divides financial theory into:

  • Ancient Period (pre-1950): From Fibonacci (1202) through Williams (1938) and early Markowitz. 98 pages.
  • Classical Period (1950-1980): Arrow-Debreu, Modigliani-Miller, Markowitz, Sharpe/CAPM, Black-Scholes, Ross APT. 210 pages.
  • Modern Period (post-1980): Behavioral finance, term structure models, derivative pricing extensions. 40 pages.

This periodization is itself a statement: the "classical" period of mathematical formalization (1950-1980) is the intellectual center of gravity. The "ancient" period is admirable precursor work; the "modern" period is refinement. This structure reflects the NPV paradigm's view of history: pre-MM practitioners had good intuitions but no rigorous theory; post-MM academics formalized everything correctly.

The Preface: Merton's Naming Paradox and Attribution Errors

Rubinstein opens with Merton's "Stigler's Law of Eponymy" (discoveries are rarely named after their true originators). His example:

"the capital structure irrelevance proposition had its inception in a single paragraph contained in a now rarely referenced but amazing book by John Burr Williams published in 1938, 20 years before Modigliani-Miller. Had his initial insight been well known and carefully considered, we might have been spared decades of confusion."

And:

"A clear example of Merton's naming paradox is the 'Gordon growth formula.' Unfortunately, once this type of error takes hold, it is very difficult to shake loose."

This is a remarkable admission: major results of the "Classical Period" were anticipated by "Ancient Period" practitioners, and the field gave them wrong names. The EPS project extends this observation: not just individual results but the entire NPV framework had deep pre-MM roots in earnings capitalization practice.

Graham and Dodd (1934): Nearly Discovered MM — Then Turned Away

Rubinstein's treatment of Graham and Dodd is one of the book's most revealing passages. He shows that Graham & Dodd (1934) compared three firms with identical operating cash flows but different capital structures:

Firm Earnings to Stock Value of Stock Value of Bonds Total Value
A $1,000,000 $10,000,000 — $10,000,000
B $750,000 $7,500,000 $5,000,000 $12,500,000
C $500,000 $5,000,000 $10,000,000 $15,000,000

Bonds pay 5%; stocks capitalize earnings at 10:1 (i.e., 10% cap rate = the earnings yield).

Graham and Dodd recognized the paradox: same operating cash flows, different total values due to leverage. They even showed that Firm A stock should be worth $12.5M by a no-arbitrage argument — nearly the MM proof. But then, as Rubinstein notes:

"Unfortunately, Graham and Dodd, now on the verge of discovering one of the most important ideas in the history of investments, in the very next sentence turn away from this promising direction."

They retreated because they believed the arbitrage would not work in practice (limited liability, market imperfections). This passage documents: (1) practitioners in 1934 used earnings capitalization at the 10% earnings yield; (2) they were aware of capital structure implications; (3) they did not follow through to formal irrelevance because institutional knowledge told them leverage does matter in practice.

Williams (1938): Capital Structure Irrelevance Before MM

Rubinstein confirms that Williams (1938) derived the full MM capital structure irrelevance proposition:

"the capital structure irrelevance proposition had its inception in a single paragraph contained in a now rarely referenced but amazing book by John Burr Williams published in 1938."

Williams' "Law of Conservation of Investment Value" (see our Williams research note) is MM Proposition I, 20 years early. This is historically accurate and consequential — the formal mathematical result that MM (1958) "discovered" was already present in Williams (1938), yet the field forgot it.

The Ancient Period: What Rubinstein Covers

Key works annotated in the Ancient Period relevant to the EPS project:

  • Fibonacci (1202) — compound interest and present value calculations
  • De la Vega (1688) — first description of a stock exchange (Amsterdam)
  • Bachelier (1900) — random walk of prices (options pricing forerunner)
  • Fisher (1907, 1930) — theory of interest and present value (mathematical foundation of DCF)
  • Keynes (1936) — animal spirits and speculative markets
  • Graham and Dodd (1934/1940) — security analysis and earnings-based valuation
  • Williams (1938) — dividend discount model and capital structure irrelevance
  • Markowitz (1952) — mean-variance portfolio theory (beginning of the Classical Period)

Notably absent: Dewing (1920), Guthmann & Dougall, Mead, Solomon, Dean. The entire dominant corporate finance textbook tradition of earnings capitalization is not covered. Rubinstein's history is a history of mathematical ideas, not of business practice. The practitioner tradition that the EPS project studies is systematically invisible to him.

The Classical Period: The Formalization That Displaced Earnings Capitalization

Rubinstein's Classical Period (1950-1980) covers the works the EPS project identifies as the "paradigm shift":

  • Modigliani-Miller (1958, 1963) — capital structure irrelevance, tax shields
  • Markowitz (1952, 1959) — portfolio selection
  • Sharpe (1964) / CAPM — cost of equity via beta, replacing the earnings yield
  • Black-Scholes-Merton (1973) — options pricing
  • Fama (1965, 1970) — efficient markets
  • Ross (1976) — Arbitrage Pricing Theory

The Hamada (1969) annotation is particularly relevant: Hamada found that "dividend payout, financial leverage, and measures of earnings yield instability not only are correlated with beta, but can predict the next period's beta better than the last period's beta." This means the earnings yield has empirical content for predicting systematic risk — a bridge between the two paradigms.

Rubinstein's Implicit Framework: Mathematical Rigor as the Standard

Rubinstein's selection criterion is explicit: he covers works with mathematical rigor and formal proofs. The consequences for intellectual history:

  1. Pre-1950 practitioner uses of earnings capitalization are invisible — no formulas, no note
  2. The intellectual genealogy runs Fisher → Williams → MM → CAPM, skipping the parallel practitioner tradition
  3. "Discovery" is attributed to the first formal mathematical proof, not to earlier informal uses

This is Rubinstein's own version of the naming paradox: DCF is attributed to Fisher (1907) and Williams (1938), even though earnings capitalization practitioners were doing equivalent calculations (in the zero-growth case) for decades before Fisher published.

Significance

1. Most Comprehensive Intellectual History of Academic Finance

For the EPS project, Rubinstein's book confirms the academic intellectual genealogy from Fisher to Williams to MM and shows exactly which pre-1950 works the academic tradition chose to remember. It is the "official" account the EPS project argues is incomplete.

2. The Pre-MM Practitioner Tradition Is Systematically Absent

Rubinstein's Ancient Period (98 pages) focuses on mathematical works. The dominant corporate finance textbook tradition — Dewing, Guthmann, Mead — is entirely absent. This confirms the EPS project's thesis: the practitioner tradition was so thoroughly displaced that even a comprehensive history of financial theory does not discuss it.

3. Graham & Dodd Used Earnings Capitalization at 10x Multiple in 1934

Rubinstein's Graham & Dodd passage documents that in 1934, practitioners valued stocks using a 10:1 earnings multiple (10% capitalization rate). This is the same formula as Dewing (1920) and Guthmann & Dougall (1940). The 10% earnings yield as the cost of equity was the standard practitioner method throughout the pre-MM era.

4. Williams (1938) = MM Proposition I — Confirmed by a Skeptic of Practitioner Finance

Rubinstein, who was himself a mathematical finance theorist, explicitly confirms that Williams (1938) contained MM capital structure irrelevance. This is not a sympathetic reading by a pro-practitioner historian — it is an admission by an academic insider that the practitioners got there first.

5. The Naming Paradox Extends to the EPS vs. NPV Debate

If the "Gordon growth formula" is really Williams (1938), and if "MM capital structure irrelevance" is really Williams (1938), then perhaps "NPV/DCF maximization" as the firm's objective is a renamed version of what earnings capitalization practitioners were already doing — just formalized and restricted to the case where the capitalization rate equals the theoretical cost of equity. This is precisely the EPS project's argument.

6. Practical Use: Discovery Tool for Our Collection

Every work Rubinstein annotates in his Ancient Period is a candidate for our EPS Literature collection. His bibliography should be systematically checked against our holdings to identify gaps.

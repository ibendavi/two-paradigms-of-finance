Speculative Bubbles and the Greater Fool: A Theory from Pre-1960 Sources

I. The Puzzle

Why would a rational person pay more for an asset than he believes it is worth? This question --- trivial to a neoclassical economist, who would simply answer "he wouldn't" --- is the central problem in the theory of speculative bubbles. Two and a half centuries of financial writing, from the Amsterdam stock exchange to the General Theory, offer a remarkably consistent answer: because he expects to sell it to someone else for even more.

This lecture note reconstructs the theory of speculative bubbles from six pre-1960 primary sources. The theory that emerges is richer and more psychologically sophisticated than the rational-bubble models that would follow in the 1980s. It addresses not only why bubbles form but who enters, who exits, and what determines the timing.

II. Three Classes of Market Participant

The earliest surviving analysis of speculative market structure comes from Joseph de la Vega's Confusion de Confusiones (1688), a dialogue describing the Amsterdam Stock Exchange and trading in shares of the Dutch East India Company (VOC). De la Vega identifies three classes of participant:

The first class --- the "princes of business" --- hold shares for dividends. They are indifferent to price fluctuations and function as what we would now call fundamental investors. The second class consists of merchants who hold for price appreciation alongside dividends --- they are investors with a speculative overlay. The third class are pure speculators, who "have tried to decide all by themselves about the magnitude of their gains and, in order to do so, they have put up wheels of fortune" (First Dialogue, p. 6). They have entirely abandoned any pretense of fundamental valuation.

This tripartite classification --- fundamental investor, informed speculator, pure gambler --- reappears in every subsequent treatment. Crump (1887) distinguishes "bona fide investors" from "speculators" from "the public." Keynes (1936) separates "enterprise" (forecasting the prospective yield of assets over their whole life) from "speculation" (forecasting the psychology of the market). The bubble problem arises when the third class comes to dominate the market.

III. The Greater Fool Theorem

Keynes states the theorem most precisely:

It is not sensible to pay 25 for an investment of which you believe the prospective yield to justify a value of 30, if you also believe that the market will value it at 20 three months hence. (General Theory, p. 155)

The converse --- which Keynes leaves implicit but which drives the logic of every bubble --- is: it is rational to pay 30 for something you believe is worth only 25, if you expect the market to value it at 35 in three months. What matters is not the asset's fundamental value but the anticipated direction of the convention.

The key word is "convention." Keynes argues that investors, faced with radical uncertainty about the distant future, adopt a tacit social agreement: "The essence of this convention lies in assuming that the existing state of affairs will continue indefinitely, except in so far as we have specific reasons to expect a change" (p. 152). This convention has no philosophical warrant --- "our existing knowledge does not provide a sufficient basis for a calculated mathematical expectation" --- but it is useful because it provides a common basis for valuation that makes the market function.

The convention makes investment "safe for the individual investor over short periods, and hence over a succession of short periods however many, if he can fairly rely on there being no breakdown in the convention and on his therefore having an opportunity to revise his judgment" (p. 153). The investor does not need to be right about the long run; he only needs to be right about what the market will think in the short run, and to exit before the convention shifts.

IV. The Beauty Contest: Higher-Order Beliefs

This reasoning leads to Keynes's most famous analogy. In a newspaper competition, contestants pick the six prettiest faces from 100 photographs, and the prize goes to the contestant whose choices most nearly match the average preferences of all contestants:

It is not a case of choosing those which, to the best of one's judgment, are really the prettiest, nor even those which average opinion genuinely thinks the prettiest. We have reached the third degree where we devote our intelligences to anticipating what average opinion expects the average opinion to be. And there are some, I believe, who practise the fourth, fifth and higher degrees. (General Theory, pp. 155--156)

The beauty contest captures the essential structure of speculative pricing. At level 0, you estimate fundamental value --- the discounted stream of future dividends, the replacement cost of assets, the earning power of the enterprise. At level 1, you estimate what the market believes fundamental value to be. At level 2, you estimate what the market believes the market believes. And so on. The speculator who thinks at level $n+1$ has an advantage over the one who thinks at level $n$, because he can anticipate and front-run the other's trades.

In a bubble, the higher-order beliefs detach entirely from level 0. Nobody is thinking about fundamental value anymore; everyone is trying to guess what everyone else will do next. The price becomes a self-referential object, justified only by the expectation that it will be higher tomorrow.

This insight was formalized much later (Townsend 1983, Allen, Morris and Shin 2006), but Keynes's verbal formulation is more powerful than the subsequent mathematical treatments, because it emphasizes the infinite regress --- there is no natural stopping point, no level at which the reasoning terminates.

V. Anticipation and Discounting: Who Buys and Who Sells

A crucial distinction runs through the entire pre-1960 literature: the difference between trading on anticipation and trading on realization. The distinction determines who wins and who loses in speculative markets.

De la Vega, writing in 1688, identified the principle: "The expectation of an event creates a much deeper impression upon the exchange than the event itself. When large dividends or rich imports are expected, shares will rise in price; but if the expectation becomes a reality, the shares often fall" (Second Dialogue, p. 14). This is the earliest known statement of "buy the rumor, sell the news."

Emery (1896) restated it as a general principle of organized speculation: "Events are anticipated and exert their influence before they arrive. It is often surprising to see how absolutely without effect is the final occurrence of an event of importance, provided it has been expected. It is all epitomized in the familiar saying that 'Wall Street discounts everything'" (p. 117).

Selden (1912) turned this into a theory of the entry decision: "It is axiomatic that inexperienced traders and investors, and indeed a majority of the more experienced as well, are continually trying to speculate on past events" (Chapter IV, p. 43). The public buys after prices have already risen --- after the good news has already been discounted --- and interprets the rise itself as evidence that still more is to come. The professional, by contrast, "buys on anticipation of the event and sells when the event arrives" (p. 45). The public enters at the top because it confuses realized good news with anticipated good news.

This is the mechanism by which the third class --- the uninformed public --- becomes the bag-holder. Crump put it bluntly: "The professional speculative element in the community sniffs this movement on the part of the public with the accuracy of a pointer that has found his birds, and they commence to draw the credulous by fictitious prices, now and then unloading to be ready when the relapse comes" (Chapter II, p. 32).

VI. The Entry Decision: Why People Pile In

What draws people into a speculative market? The sources identify five mechanisms:

1. Low interest rates and the reach for yield. De la Vega observes that in 1680s Amsterdam, "the rate of interest on ordinary loans amounts to only 3 per cent a year... even the wealthiest men are forced to buy stocks" (p. 13). When safe returns are negligible, the opportunity cost of speculation falls. People who would never gamble at 5% will gamble at 1%. This is the oldest identified driver of speculative entry, and it remains the most robust.

2. Social proof and the visibility of gains. Mackay's account of the Mississippi Scheme (1719--1720) is driven almost entirely by the spectacle of neighbors getting rich. "The highest and the lowest classes were alike filled with a vision of boundless wealth" (p. 28). A cobbler in the Rue de Quincampoix earned 200 livres per day by lending his back as a writing-desk for speculators signing contracts. Apartments near the exchange rented at sixteen times their normal rate (pp. 28--29). When enrichment is visible --- when your coachman has made more in a month than you earn in a year --- the psychic cost of staying out exceeds the financial cost of getting in.

3. The apparent symmetry of up and down. Crump identifies a cognitive error that makes speculation seem like a fair bet: "There are only two ways for a price to move --- up and down. At first sight the chances seem to be as much in his favour as against" (Introduction, p. 8). The novice reasons that if there is a 50-50 chance of a 10% move, the expected value is zero --- a fair gamble. But this ignores transaction costs, the contango on leveraged positions, the asymmetry between riding winners (which is easy) and cutting losers (which requires active decision), and the information advantage of professionals. Crump's point is that the appearance of symmetry is enough to draw people in; the reality of asymmetry is what takes their money.

4. Seasonal and affective states. Crump, writing in 1887, makes what is probably the first documented observation of what behavioral finance would later call weather-driven risk tolerance: "There are periods of the year when the temper of investors tends to sulkiness, in sympathy with a fall of the mercury. Dull and disagreeable weather, as a rule, adversely affects the Stock markets" (Chapter II, pp. 30--31). Conversely, "as the spring comes in, with its delights and young verdure... the disposition becomes more general to disregard those strict lines of prudence" (p. 32). The bull market is partly a phenomenon of mood.

5. The ruin-and-return cycle. De la Vega documents that even after catastrophic losses, speculators re-enter the market within months: "Sometimes before six months have passed, those persons whose money was taken away from them make deals again... gullibility and seduction cannot in any way be prevented" (First Dialogue, p. 6). The psychological mechanism is a variant of loss aversion: having lost money in the market, the fastest way to recover it feels like re-entering the market. Crump describes the terminal phase: "A loss incurred through hasty ill-matured operations renders him impatient to recover it, which probably leads to its being doubled... he goes on blindly tempting fortune, only to experience ups and downs which unsettle his judgment more and more, until he begins to 'plunge,' when all is soon over" (Chapter III, p. 43).

VII. The Exit Decision: Why People Fail to Sell

If entry is overdetermined, exit is under-determined. "Any jackass can take a profit," observes Crump, "but it requires a devilish clever fellow to cut a loss" (p. 38). The pre-1960 literature identifies three reasons why participants stay too long:

1. Confirmation bias and prior commitment. Selden documents that a person's market position distorts his interpretation of new information: "It is almost invariable rule that the man who was long before this condition arose will interpret technical condition as bullish, while the man who was and remains short, sees plain indications of technical weakness" (p. 65). The long investor reads every piece of news as confirming his position; the short seller reads the same news as confirming his. Neither is reasoning from the evidence; both are reasoning from their portfolio.

2. Career risk and conventional failure. Keynes argues that even professional investors who correctly identify overvaluation cannot afford to sell. "Worldly wisdom teaches that it is better for reputation to fail conventionally than to succeed unconventionally" (p. 158). The fund manager who sells early and watches the market rise further will lose clients to the fund manager who stayed in. Being right three months early is commercially indistinguishable from being wrong. "Life is not long enough; --- human nature desires quick results" (p. 157). This is the ancestor of what would later be called "limits to arbitrage."

3. Musical chairs. Keynes's other metaphor is the game of musical chairs --- or Old Maid: "These games can be played with zest and enjoyment, though all the players know that it is the Old Maid which is circulating, or that when the music stops some of the players will find themselves unseated" (pp. 155--156). Each player believes he is quick enough to find a chair. The game is not about fundamental value; it is about relative speed. And since every player believes he is faster than average, the game continues until the music stops.

VIII. Leverage and Credit: The Amplifier

Every account of bubbles identifies credit as the mechanism that transforms speculative enthusiasm into systemic fragility. The structure varies across centuries, but the logic is constant: leverage allows small amounts of capital to control large positions, amplifying both the upside (which attracts entry) and the downside (which turns correction into panic).

De la Vega describes the 1680s Amsterdam mechanism: at each month's settlement (rescontre), a buyer could hypothecate his newly acquired shares at 80% of their value, meaning he needed only 20% equity to carry a position. Options ("opsies") provided still greater leverage without any capital commitment at all (pp. 7--8).

Crump describes the 19th-century London and New York mechanisms: the contango system, in which a bull can carry an unsettled position from one settlement to the next by paying a financing charge. His worked numerical examples show that friction costs --- contango, commissions, stamp duty --- destroy expected value on small price moves (pp. 20--22). In the U.S., margin requirements were as low as 10% of par value, meaning 10:1 leverage was standard (p. 28).

Mackay describes the most extreme case --- the Mississippi Scheme --- in which the central bank itself financed the bubble. The Banque Royale issued paper money to fund purchases of Mississippi Company shares; rising share prices were used to justify further money creation. "The higher the price of Indian and Mississippi stock, the more billets de banque were issued to keep pace with it" (p. 41). The feedback loop was explicit and institutional.

Emery makes a subtle point about the endogeneity of leverage costs. The interest rate on continuation contracts --- the cost of carrying a leveraged position --- is "determined by the state of the money market and the state of the market for the particular security" (p. 91). This means that leverage is cheapest precisely when it is most dangerous: during the boom, when lenders are confident and the money market is easy. The cost of carry falls as prices rise, encouraging still more leverage, which drives prices higher still.

Keynes ties this into his broader theory through the concept of the "state of credit" --- an independent factor distinct from speculative confidence. Both must hold for a boom to continue, but the failure of either is sufficient to trigger collapse. "A collapse in the price of equities... may have been due to the weakening either of speculative confidence or of the state of credit. But whereas the weakening of either is enough to cause a collapse, recovery requires the revival of both" (p. 158). This asymmetry --- easy to break, hard to restore --- is why busts are sharper and faster than booms.

IX. Short Sellers and Stabilization

Is speculation always destabilizing? Emery (1896) mounts a sophisticated defense of organized speculation --- meaning speculation on formal exchanges with short-selling, margin requirements, and transparent price discovery --- as a stabilizing force.

The key mechanism is short sellers. "If it were not for strong short selling when the market becomes inflated, prices might rise to almost any extent before the final crash. Now the rise tends to be checked by the efforts of shrewd operators to take advantage of the inflation" (p. 121). Short sellers provide a counterweight to unchecked optimism; they are the bears who lean against the wind.

Emery compares organized and unorganized markets. Real estate, which has no short-selling and no continuous price discovery, exhibits wilder swings: "Compare the situation during a real estate boom. Here only the sanguine affect the price on the rise, and only the gloomy on the fall. At one end prices are more recklessly inflated, and at the other more needlessly depressed, than would be possible in an organized speculative market" (p. 121).

He backs this with data. Cotton price volatility across three eras: in the pre-speculative period (1821--1830), the average annual price fluctuation was 40.79%; in the intermediate period (1851--1860), 25.41%; in the fully speculative period (1885--1894), when organized futures markets were mature, 18.83% (pp. 124--129). Organized speculation, far from increasing volatility, appears to have reduced it by roughly half.

This is also, however, a case for limits. Emery defines speculation precisely: "the attempt to make money out of fluctuations in the value of property, as distinct from its earnings" (quoting Hadley, p. 96). When speculation serves to discover future prices, it is socially productive. When it becomes purely self-referential --- when traders bet on each other's bets rather than on the prospective value of goods --- it degenerates into gambling. The question for any bubble is where the line lies.

X. An Eight-Phase Model of the Bubble

Synthesizing all six sources, the following chronological structure emerges:

Phase 1: Fundamental demand. Low interest rates make equities attractive relative to bonds and deposits (de la Vega's reach-for-yield). Real economic growth creates genuine value; early price increases are justified by fundamentals (Emery's accurate price discovery).

Phase 2: Informed entry and discounting. Sophisticated investors --- de la Vega's second class, Crump's professional speculators --- buy on anticipated good news. Prices rise in advance of events. The market "discounts everything" (Emery, p. 117). At this stage, prices may still be near or below fundamental value.

Phase 3: Public contagion and social proof. Observable enrichment of early entrants draws the general public (Mackay). Spring psychology and rising confidence reduce risk perception (Crump). The public enters by speculating on past events --- on the price rise itself rather than on its causes (Selden). Professional operators exploit public entry by "unloading" to newcomers (Crump, p. 32).

Phase 4: Leverage amplification. Contango, margin, and hypothecation enable leveraged carry at low cost (Crump, de la Vega). Credit costs fall endogenously as lender confidence peaks and the money market eases (Emery, Keynes). Borrowed money enters the market, pushing prices beyond any level that fundamental analysis can justify.

Phase 5: Pure greater-fool dynamics. The convention holds prices above fundamentals because each investor expects to exit in time (Keynes). The beauty contest dominates: level-2 and level-3 thinking drives trading decisions. Prior commitments distort perception of new information (Selden). No one is analyzing dividends anymore.

Phase 6: Smart-money exit. Professionals and insiders begin to sell --- quietly, in small quantities, so as not to move the market. Mackay documents this in the Mississippi Scheme: "Shrewd operators... quietly, and in small quantities at a time, converted their notes into specie, and sent it away to foreign countries" (p. 42). Short sellers accumulate positions (Emery). The exit is gradual and invisible to the public.

Phase 7: Trigger and cascade. A triggering event occurs. It need not be large --- Keynes emphasizes that it need only disturb the convention, which was always fragile. In the Mississippi Scheme, the Prince de Conti demanded specie for 20 million livres of paper (Mackay, p. 41), which was a small fraction of the outstanding notes but enough to expose the emperor's nakedness. Forced liquidation of leveraged positions amplifies the crash (Crump). Both speculative confidence and credit confidence collapse simultaneously (Keynes).

Phase 8: Aftermath and re-entry. Within six months, ruined speculators return to the market (de la Vega). The system is self-renewing because the function of bearing risk is genuinely necessary to commerce (Emery): goods must be stored, crops must be hedged, capital must be allocated. The same institutional infrastructure that enables productive risk-transfer also enables speculative excess. There is no way to have one without the other.

XI. What Determines Whether a Person Piles In, Sells, or Stays Out

We can now answer the lecture's central question. The decision depends on three variables:

1. The individual's position in the information hierarchy. Professionals who trade on anticipation (level-2 thinkers in Keynes's framework) enter early and exit before the public arrives. The public, trading on realization (level-0 or level-1 thinkers), enters late and is left holding the bag. The decision to enter or stay out is largely a function of what level of the beauty contest the individual is playing.

2. The individual's existing commitments. Selden's insight is that once you have a position, your perception of the market changes to conform to your position rather than the other way around. The decision to sell is not made on the same informational basis as the decision to buy. Prior commitment creates a powerful bias toward holding --- and the larger the unrealized loss, the stronger the bias, because selling requires acknowledging that the loss is real.

3. The social and career costs of nonparticipation. Keynes's argument about conventional failure explains why even sophisticated investors who recognize overvaluation may rationally choose to participate. The fund manager who exits early and is proven "wrong" for six months loses his job. The fund manager who stays in and loses money alongside everyone else keeps his reputation intact. In a bubble, the social rewards of conformity exceed the financial rewards of independent judgment.

The model has a tragic structure. At each moment, each individual is making a locally rational decision --- the professional who buys on anticipation, the merchant who follows the professional, the public that follows the merchant, the leveraged speculator who rolls his position forward because the financing is cheap. No one is making a mistake, given their information set and incentive structure. Yet the collective outcome is a catastrophe. The bubble is not a failure of individual rationality; it is a failure of aggregation.

XII. Keynes's Casino

The deepest concern in the pre-1960 literature is not that bubbles make some people poor. It is that they distort the allocation of real resources. Keynes:

Speculators may do no harm as bubbles on a steady stream of enterprise. But the position is serious when enterprise becomes the bubble on a whirlpool of speculation. When the capital development of a country becomes a by-product of the activities of a casino, the job is likely to be ill-done. (General Theory, p. 159)

The casino metaphor captures a structural point. In a well-functioning market, enterprise --- the activity of forecasting the prospective yield of assets over their whole life --- drives prices. Speculation is a minor overlay, adding liquidity and absorbing risk. In a bubble, the relationship inverts: speculation drives prices, and enterprise becomes irrelevant. Capital flows not to the most productive uses but to the most speculative ones.

This is why Keynes, somewhat heretically for a man who made a fortune speculating, proposed a "substantial Government transfer tax on all transactions" to "mitigate the predominance of speculation over enterprise" (pp. 159--160). By reducing liquidity, the tax would raise the cost of short-term trading and shift the balance back toward long-term investment. He even contemplated making investments "permanent and indissoluble, like marriage" --- acknowledging immediately that this cure might be worse than the disease.

The pre-1960 literature, taken as a whole, does not offer a complete formal model of bubbles. What it offers is something arguably more valuable: a richly textured phenomenology of speculative behavior, grounded in centuries of observation, that identifies the mechanisms of entry, contagion, leverage, exit failure, and collapse with remarkable consistency across very different institutional settings. The Amsterdam exchange of 1688, the Paris of 1720, the London and New York of the 1880s, and the interwar markets that Keynes knew all exhibited the same fundamental dynamics. That consistency is itself an important datum.

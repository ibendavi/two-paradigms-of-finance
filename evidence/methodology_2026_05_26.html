Academic The Great Divergence (1964–present) • 2026

Methodology: Measuring Academic-Practitioner Relevance in Finance, 1870–2025

1. Research question

How has the relevance of academic finance scholarship to financial practice evolved over time, and is the post-1960 academic mainstream — formal modeling under risk-neutral expectations, market efficiency, agency theory, etc. — empirically connected to the methods practitioners actually use?

Working hypothesis (to be tested, not assumed):

  • H1 (Convergence-then-divergence): Pre-1960 academic and practitioner finance share concepts, methods, and vocabulary. Starting in the 1960s (Markowitz, Modigliani-Miller, Sharpe), the academic mainstream diverges. Practitioners continue to use a stable, slowly evolving toolkit anchored in pre-1960 thinking, augmented by technology-enabled mechanics (spreadsheets, Black-Scholes pricers) but not by the academic conceptual frame.
  • H2 (Selective adoption): Some academic ideas crossed over (Black-Scholes, DCF formalization, beta, real options); most did not. The crossover rate has slowed over time.
  • H3 (Translation bridges): Academic-to-practitioner translation outlets (HBR, FAJ, JoF practitioner reviews) shrink in coverage of frontier academic work post-1980. Translation efficacy is the leading indicator of academic relevance.

These hypotheses are conjectures, not findings. The methodology must be capable of falsifying H1 — e.g., it should be able to find no break in 1960, or to find a break elsewhere (e.g., 1973 with Black-Scholes, or 1990 with behavioral finance).

2. Why this is hard to measure

Eight first-order problems any design must address. These are the real constraints; everything below is shaped by them.

# Problem Implication
1 Vocabulary drift — "leverage" in 1900 ≠ 1990. The language itself evolves. Cannot use 2020-era keyword lists to score 1900-era texts. Period-anchored vocabularies required.
2 Volume asymmetry — academic publication 50× larger by 2020 than 1900; practitioner publication grew but slower. All measures must be rate-based or per-text-normalized, not absolute counts.
3 Selection / survivorship — pre-1900 surviving practitioner texts are biased toward consequential or famous works. Pre-1900 conclusions can be descriptive, not inferential. Inferential analysis confined to ≥1900.
4 Hybrid texts — HBR articles are academic-authored but practitioner-targeted. CFA curricula are practitioner-authored but academic-flavored. Cannot impose binary academic/practitioner labels at the text level; must score on a continuous spectrum.
5 Technology shocks — DCF impractical before spreadsheets; option pricing impractical before computers. Adoption-of-academic-method tests must control for technology enablers. Method absence in 1955 doesn't imply rejection.
6 Coverage holes — corpus heavier on certain decades and journals than others. Practitioner side especially thin pre-1920. Source-level fixed effects. Always-included anchor sources for both sides.
7 Causal identification — does academic work influence practice, or does practice motivate academic work, or do both respond to a third factor (market events)? The methodology can measure co-evolution and adoption timing. Causal claims require natural experiments or instruments and should be stated carefully.
8 What counts as "useful"? — Conceptual fluency? Method adoption? Citation? Prediction? Multiple dependent variables, not one. Triangulation across them is the design's main strength.

4. Five empirical strategies (complementary, not exclusive)

Presented in order of conceptual ambition. The strongest paper uses A + B + C as core; D + E as robustness/triangulation.

Strategy A — Paradigm Spectrum (continuous classification)

Idea. Every text gets a score in [−1, +1]: −1 = purely academic, +1 = purely practitioner. Score is derived from embeddings against two-pole reference corpora (already implemented in Periodicals/analyze_trajectories_v2.py). The spectrum lets us locate HBR, FAJ, CFA, etc., without forcing binary labels.

What it measures. Position on the academic↔practitioner axis as a function of decade and topic.

Empirical content.

  • Trace mean paradigm score of HBR articles by decade. Hypothesis: drifts academic post-1970 then stalls.
  • Distribution of all academic-authored articles by decade. Hypothesis: variance shrinks (academy becomes more uniform).
  • Distribution of all practitioner texts by decade. Hypothesis: stable mean and variance.
  • Distance between the two distributions (Wasserstein or KL) over time. Hypothesis: monotonically increasing post-1960.

Strengths. Resolves the hybrid-text problem cleanly. Already partially implemented.

Weaknesses. Embedding models trained on modern text may misrepresent 19th-century language. Need period-stratified training or a contemporaneity check.

Strategy B — Topic-Specific Concept Adoption Curves

Idea. Pick a topic (M&A as pilot). Build a topic-vocabulary: 50–200 concepts/methods/named-theories within M&A. For each concept, find first appearance in any text, then trace its diffusion through academic and practitioner corpora separately as a function of year.

M&A example concept list (illustrative, not final):

  • Method: DCF, accretion/dilution analysis, comparable transactions, real options on growth, leveraged buyout structuring, M&A arbitrage, fairness opinion
  • Concept: synergy, hubris, agency cost of free cash flow, winner's curse, market-for-corporate-control, anti-trust capital structure
  • Theory: event-study methodology, abnormal returns, target-acquirer wealth division
  • Practitioner-only: "deal heat", "strategic premium", "scale rationale", "white knight", "poison pill"

Per concept, measure:

  • First appearance year (academic / practitioner side, separately)
  • Adoption lag = years from first academic appearance to ≥1% prevalence in practitioner corpus (and vice versa)
  • Peak prevalence year and decay
  • Cross-side correlation in usage time series

What we test.

  • Does pre-1960 cross-side lag shorten relative to post-1960?
  • Are there concepts academia invented post-1960 with effectively zero practitioner uptake? (Predicted: yes — pricing kernels, factor zoo, identification-via-shock papers.)
  • Are there concepts practitioners invented or maintained that academia ignores? (Predicted: yes — deal heat, accretion/dilution, strategic premium narratives.)

Strengths. Topic-specific, falsifiable, interpretable concepts that domain experts (you) can vet. Maps directly to "what is being taught vs what is being done."

Weaknesses. Concept list construction is labor-intensive and biases the result. Mitigation: build the list from period-stratified textbook tables of contents — a structural anchor harder to game.

Strategy C — Structural Break Tests on Cross-Side Distance

Idea. Once strategies A and B yield time series (paradigm-distance series, concept-overlap rates, adoption-lag series), apply standard break-point econometrics — Bai-Perron multiple structural breaks, Quandt-Andrews supF — to identify the data-revealed turning points without pre-imposing 1960.

What it tests. The H1 hypothesis directly. If 1960 is right, the test should locate the break in 1959–1968 with high posterior. If the break is at 1973 (Black-Scholes / oil shock) or 1990 (rise of behavioral / asset-pricing factor zoo), the test must say so.

Strengths. Disciplined inference. Tells you when the break actually happened.

Weaknesses. Requires sufficient pre-break observations. Pre-1900 is thin enough that we may want to bound the search at 1900.

Strategy D — HBR Translation Efficacy

Idea. HBR's stated mission is to translate academic management/finance research for executives. Quantify how well it does.

Operationalization.

  • For each academic-finance concept c that appears in the JoF/JFE/RFS corpus in year t, ask: does c appear in HBR within years [t, t+5]?
  • Compute the time series of "translation rate" = P(concept appears in HBR within 5y | concept appears in top-3 academic journals in year t).
  • Decline in this rate post-1980 → academic finance produces less translatable content. Alternative interpretation: HBR's editorial priorities shifted away from finance toward strategy/leadership; this would itself confirm divergence.

Strengths. Tests the mechanism directly. HBR is the natural "academy talks to practitioners" channel.

Weaknesses. HBR has finite article slots. May reflect editorial choices, not academic relevance. Cross-check with FAJ (which CFA Institute publishes — pure practitioner editorial gate).

Strategy E — Earnings-Call Anchor Test (post-1995 only)

Idea. Earnings call transcripts are the cleanest evidence we have of what CFOs actually say about their financial decisions. Use them as a downstream "ground truth" of practitioner method.

Operationalization.

  • Build a CFO-language vocabulary from the 540K transcripts.
  • For each academic concept in a given topic, measure its prevalence in CFO speech by year.
  • Cross-validate with the practitioner-text measure. Concepts adopted in practitioner texts but not in CFO speech are publishing-side fashion; concepts in both are real adoption.

Strengths. Independent measurement of practice. Catches the "practitioners pay lip service but don't actually use it" failure mode.

Weaknesses. Earnings calls are managed communications, not internal practice. Post-1995 only.

5. Pilot — M&A subset (recommended starting point)

M&A is the right pilot because:

  • Both communities have produced thick text streams on the topic for 100+ years
  • The method evolution is rich and somewhat datable: 1900s/1920s era trust/merger waves, 1960s conglomerate wave, 1980s LBO era, 1990s mega-merger era, 2000s private equity, 2010s tech roll-ups
  • Easy to define topic boundaries (filter on M&A-related vocab in first N pages)
  • Pivotal academic theories (Jensen 1986 free cash flow; Myers-Majluf 1984; event-study methodology 1969) are crisply datable

Concrete first deliverable:

  1. Build the M&A subcorpus by filtering each text/article for M&A-vocabulary density ≥ threshold
  2. Manually construct ~80-concept M&A taxonomy from top 10 academic + top 10 practitioner M&A texts (period-balanced)
  3. Build adoption curves per concept (Strategy B)
  4. Run paradigm-spectrum on the M&A subset (Strategy A)
  5. Identify break points (Strategy C)
  6. Write a 15-page draft. Use it to decide whether to scale to other topics.

If the M&A pilot supports H1, the same machinery extends to: capital structure, valuation/cost-of-capital, payout, governance, risk management, derivatives. If it doesn't support H1, the design itself is at risk and we revise before scaling.

6. What is not in the design — and why

  • Citation graphs are tempting but unevenly available pre-1990 and rare in practitioner texts. Treat citations as bonus evidence, not load-bearing.
  • Author-network analysis (who-cites-whom, who-coauthors-whom) is interesting but answers a different question (academic sociology, not practitioner relevance). Defer.
  • Topic models (LDA/BERTopic) are useful for exploratory work but their stochastic outputs are hard to defend in a top-journal paper. Use them to suggest the concept list, not to be the concept list.
  • Sentiment analysis distracts from the concept question.
  • Citations to specific seminal papers (e.g., "how many practitioners cite Modigliani-Miller?") are too narrow to drive the paper but make good case-study sidebars.

7. Threats to validity (preview of paper §6)

  • Reverse causation. Practitioner topics may motivate academic agendas (e.g., LBO wave motivated agency theory) rather than the other way. The methodology should measure co-evolution; causal claims should be confined to specific natural experiments (e.g., Black-Scholes was published, then options exchanges adopted it within months — clear directionality).
  • Confounding by financial-system events. 1929, 1973, 2008. These shocks shift both communities simultaneously. Use shock dummies; check whether the academic-practitioner gap widens or narrows around shocks.
  • Confounding by text genre composition. Academic-side composition shifts from monographs to journals over time. Practitioner-side shifts from trade manuals to magazines to blogs. Genre fixed effects.

8. Decision points I need from you

  1. Pilot topic: M&A as proposed, or different? (Capital structure has stronger theoretical lineage; valuation has more text mass.)
  2. Concept-list construction: built from textbook tables of contents (structural, defensible), or LLM-suggested then human-vetted (faster, more contested), or both?
  3. Spectrum implementation: keep current Periodicals/analyze_trajectories_v2.py two-pole embedding approach, or upgrade to a Sentence-BERT or domain-finetuned model?
  4. Earnings-call integration timing: integrate from the start (more independent variation, more compute) or after the textual pilot (cleaner first paper)?
  5. Paper structure: one omnibus paper with M&A as flagship topic + 4 robustness topics, or one paper per topic with M&A as the methodological showcase?
  6. HBR centrality: is HBR's translation rate the headline result, or a secondary check? (Argument for headline: it's the cleanest test of the mechanism. Argument against: it's one outlet, easy to dismiss as editorial.)

The Two Literatures

Topic trajectories in practitioner trade magazines vs. academic finance journals, 1865–2026.

We digitized the full text of three major Wall Street trade magazines and the titles of every article in the top three academic finance journals. For each 5-year window, we measure what fraction of documents discuss each topic. The result: two literatures that inhabit different worlds. Practitioners write about earnings, speculation, and trading. Academics write about portfolio theory, efficient markets, and risk models. The overlap is vanishingly small.

Methodology & keyword lists

Goal: Measure how the style and framing of writing about a given financial topic differs between practitioner and academic sources, and how that difference evolves over time.

  1. Topic filtering. Each source’s full text is split into ~3,000-character segments. Segments are kept only if they contain at least one keyword from the topic’s filter list. Crucially, the keywords identify what the text is about (e.g., “merger,” “dividend,” “leverage”) and deliberately exclude method-specific terms (NPV, DCF, CAPM, earnings yield, P/E ratio, etc.) so that the paradigm signal comes from the embedding, not from the keyword filter.
  2. Embedding. Each filtered segment is embedded using a sentence-transformer model (all-MiniLM-L6-v2, 384 dimensions). The first ~1,500 characters are used (model context window).
  3. Pureplay centroids. For each topic, two “pole” centroids are built from segments where the paradigm is known a priori: Pole 1: all matching segments from practitioner sources published before 1920 (before the rise of academic finance theory). Pole 2: all matching segments from academic journals published after 2000 (mature modern finance). Each centroid is the mean of its pole’s embeddings, L2-normalized.
  4. Scoring. Every segment is scored as:
    score = cos_sim(segment, practitioner_centroid) − cos_sim(segment, academic_centroid)
    Positive = closer to the pre-1920 practitioner writing style; negative = closer to the post-2000 academic writing style.
  5. Aggregation. Scores are grouped by stream (practitioner / academic) and 5-year window. The chart shows the mean score ± 2 standard errors for each stream over time.

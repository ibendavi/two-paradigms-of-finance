M&A Paradigm Vocabulary Over Time

M&A excerpts (sentence windows containing merger / acquisition / takeover / tender offer + a corporate-finance context term, ≥2 keywords) are scored by counting hits from two vocabulary dictionaries: academic-finance terms (53 patterns — CAPM, agency cost, abnormal return, regression, cumulative abnormal, market for corporate control, OLS, Tobin's Q, hubris hypothesis, asymmetric information, …) and deal-making terms (51 patterns — due diligence, accretive, fairness opinion, EBITDA multiple, poison pill, white knight, earnout, post-merger integration, …). Density = hits per 1000 characters, aggregated in 5-year windows (min 5 excerpts/window).

Why concept dictionaries? An earlier embedding-similarity approach (cosine to a pre-1930 practitioner pole and a post-2000 academic pole) confounded paradigm with era: any modern English text scored more "academic" simply because of vocabulary, OCR, and source-mix drift over time. Concept dictionaries fire on specific terms regardless of era, so a 1925 article that uses regression or standard deviation registers as academic, and a 2015 article that uses due diligence or accretive registers as practitioner.

What this shows

Pre-1960: M&A discussions in both camps used neither modern academic-finance jargon nor modern deal jargon. CFC (1880–1949) and Magazine of Wall Street wrote about mergers in plain business prose.

Post-1960: academic finance creates an entirely new technical lexicon (CAPM 1964, M&M 1958, agency theory 1976, event study 1969). Academic journals shoot from ~0.1 to ~0.7 hits/1000 chars in academic terms.

Diffusion is asymmetric. Practitioner textbooks pick up some academic vocabulary (~0.15 density) but stay practitioner-dominated. Practitioner magazines barely adopt academic vocabulary at all even today. Deal-making vocabulary develops in parallel and stays mostly inside practitioner textbooks.
